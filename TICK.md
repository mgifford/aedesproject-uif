# Tick-Borne Disease Surveillance

Ticks are obligate blood-feeding arthropods and the second most important vector of human infectious diseases worldwide, after mosquitoes. In the United States, tick-borne diseases account for more than 50,000 reported cases annually, with significant under-reporting estimated at 10x or more for diseases like Lyme.

---

## Tick Species and Their Diseases

### *Ixodes scapularis* — Blacklegged Tick (Deer Tick)
**Range**: Eastern and north-central US; expanding northward  
**Active season**: Nymphs peak May–July; adults active Oct–May (active in near-freezing temps)  
**Diseases transmitted**:
- Lyme disease (*Borrelia burgdorferi*, *B. mayonii*)
- Anaplasmosis (*Anaplasma phagocytophilum*)
- Babesiosis (*Babesia microti*, *B. duncani*)
- Powassan virus (lineage II / Deer Tick Virus)
- Borrelia miyamotoi (relapsing fever group)
- Ehrlichiosis (*Ehrlichia muris eauclairensis*)

### *Ixodes pacificus* — Western Blacklegged Tick
**Range**: Pacific Coast states (CA, OR, WA, AZ, NV, UT)  
**Active season**: Oct–May (adults); Mar–Jul (nymphs)  
**Diseases transmitted**:
- Lyme disease (*Borrelia burgdorferi*)
- Anaplasmosis (*Anaplasma phagocytophilum*)
- Borrelia miyamotoi

### *Dermacentor variabilis* — American Dog Tick
**Range**: East of the Rockies; Pacific Coast  
**Active season**: Apr–Aug  
**Diseases transmitted**:
- Rocky Mountain Spotted Fever (RMSF) (*Rickettsia rickettsii*)
- Tularemia (*Francisella tularensis*)
- Tick paralysis

### *Dermacentor andersoni* — Rocky Mountain Wood Tick
**Range**: Rocky Mountain states and into western Canada  
**Active season**: Mar–Aug (elevation-dependent)  
**Diseases transmitted**:
- Rocky Mountain Spotted Fever (*Rickettsia rickettsii*)
- Colorado Tick Fever (CTF) virus
- Tularemia (*Francisella tularensis*)
- Tick paralysis

### *Amblyomma americanum* — Lone Star Tick
**Range**: Southeastern and south-central US; expanding north and west  
**Active season**: Mar–Sep  
**Diseases transmitted**:
- Ehrlichiosis (*Ehrlichia chaffeensis*, *E. ewingii*)
- Tularemia (*Francisella tularensis*)
- Heartland virus
- Bourbon virus
- Southern tick-associated rash illness (STARI)
- Alpha-gal syndrome (red meat allergy sensitization)

### *Amblyomma maculatum* — Gulf Coast Tick
**Range**: Atlantic and Gulf Coast states  
**Active season**: Apr–Sep  
**Diseases transmitted**:
- Rickettsia parkeri rickettsiosis
- Spotted fever group rickettsioses

### *Rhipicephalus sanguineus* — Brown Dog Tick
**Range**: Nationwide (primarily indoors/kennels); high RMSF risk in AZ, NM, tribal lands  
**Active season**: Year-round indoors  
**Diseases transmitted**:
- Rocky Mountain Spotted Fever (primary vector in AZ tribal communities)
- Canine ehrlichiosis and anaplasmosis

## Disease Reference

### Lyme Disease
**Agent**: *Borrelia burgdorferi* (spirochete)  
**Vectors**: *I. scapularis*, *I. pacificus*  
**Reservoir**: White-footed mouse (*Peromyscus leucopus*), deer  
**Incubation**: 3–30 days  
**Symptoms**: Erythema migrans rash (70–80%), flu-like illness; later: arthritis, cardiac, neurological  
**Case fatality**: <1% (rare deaths from cardiac involvement)  
**Annual US cases**: ~476,000 estimated; ~63,000 reported (2022)  
**Reportable**: Nationally notifiable  
**Treatment**: Doxycycline, amoxicillin, cefuroxime (early stage)  
**Monitoring signals**: Tick drag sampling, wildlife seroprevalence, ER chief complaint data, Google Trends ("bull's eye rash"), iNaturalist *Ixodes* observations

### Rocky Mountain Spotted Fever (RMSF)
**Agent**: *Rickettsia rickettsii* (gram-negative bacterium)  
**Vectors**: *D. variabilis*, *D. andersoni*, *R. sanguineus*  
**Incubation**: 2–14 days  
**Symptoms**: Sudden high fever, headache, rash (appears day 2–4, starts wrists/ankles)  
**Case fatality**: 20–25% if untreated; <5% with prompt doxycycline  
**Annual US cases**: ~4,000–6,000 reported  
**Reportable**: Nationally notifiable  
**Critical note**: Most fatal tick-borne disease in the US; treatment must begin before lab confirmation  
**Monitoring signals**: Emergency department records, tick drag sampling, physician case reports

### Anaplasmosis
**Agent**: *Anaplasma phagocytophilum* (obligate intracellular bacterium)  
**Vectors**: *I. scapularis*, *I. pacificus*  
**Incubation**: 1–2 weeks  
**Symptoms**: Fever, headache, malaise, muscle aches; rarely neurological  
**Case fatality**: <1%  
**Annual US cases**: ~5,700 reported (2019)  
**Reportable**: Nationally notifiable  
**Co-infection**: Often co-transmitted with Lyme and Babesiosis

### Babesiosis
**Agent**: *Babesia microti*, *B. duncani* (intraerythrocytic parasites)  
**Vectors**: *I. scapularis*, *I. pacificus*  
**Incubation**: 1–9 weeks  
**Symptoms**: Hemolytic anemia, fever, fatigue; severe in immunocompromised/asplenic patients  
**Case fatality**: <1% (up to 20% in high-risk populations)  
**Annual US cases**: ~2,400 reported  
**Reportable**: Nationally notifiable  
**Blood supply risk**: Can be transfusion-transmitted

### Ehrlichiosis
**Agent**: *Ehrlichia chaffeensis*, *E. ewingii*, *E. muris eauclairensis*  
**Vectors**: *A. americanum* (*E. chaffeensis*, *E. ewingii*); *I. scapularis* (*E. muris*)  
**Incubation**: 1–2 weeks  
**Symptoms**: Similar to anaplasmosis; leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, elevated liver enzymes  
**Case fatality**: 1–3% if untreated  
**Annual US cases**: ~2,000 reported  
**Reportable**: Nationally notifiable

### Colorado Tick Fever (CTF)
**Agent**: Colorado tick fever virus (Coltivirus, dsRNA)  
**Vectors**: *D. andersoni*  
**Range**: Rocky Mountain states, 4,000–10,000 ft elevation  
**Incubation**: 1–14 days  
**Symptoms**: Biphasic fever (fever-remission-fever pattern), headache, myalgia, leukopenia  
**Case fatality**: <1% (rare encephalitis in children)  
**Reportable**: Not nationally notifiable; state-reportable in CO, UT, WY  
**Seasonality**: May–August (follows *D. andersoni* activity)

### Powassan Virus (POWV)
**Agent**: Powassan virus (Flavivirus)  
**Vectors**: *I. scapularis* (lineage II), *I. cookei*, *I. marxi*  
**Transmission time**: As fast as 15 minutes (unlike Lyme which requires 36–48 hours)  
**Incubation**: 1–5 weeks  
**Symptoms**: Encephalitis, meningitis; severe neurological sequelae  
**Case fatality**: 10–15%; ~50% of survivors have permanent neurological damage  
**Annual US cases**: ~25–50 reported (likely under-detected)  
**Reportable**: Nationally notifiable  
**Trend**: Increasing in the Northeast and Great Lakes region

### Tularemia
**Agent**: *Francisella tularensis* (gram-negative bacterium)  
**Vectors**: *D. variabilis*, *D. andersoni*, *A. americanum*; also rabbit contact, deer fly bites  
**Incubation**: 3–5 days (range 1–14)  
**Symptoms**: Ulceroglandular form most common; also pneumonic (most severe), typhoidal  
**Case fatality**: <2% with treatment; up to 30% untreated pneumonic form  
**Annual US cases**: ~200–300 reported  
**Reportable**: Nationally notifiable; potential bioterrorism agent (Category A)

### Heartland Virus
**Agent**: Heartland virus (Phlebovirus, ssRNA)  
**Vectors**: *A. americanum*  
**Range**: South-central and eastern US  
**Incubation**: Unknown  
**Symptoms**: Fever, fatigue, leukopenia, thrombocytopenia; no specific treatment  
**Case fatality**: ~10% in reported cases (may be higher)  
**Reportable**: Not nationally notifiable; under active surveillance via Emerging Infections Program

### Alpha-Gal Syndrome
**Agent**: Sensitization to galactose-alpha-1,3-galactose (a carbohydrate in mammal tissue)  
**Vector**: *A. americanum* (primary); possibly other ticks  
**Mechanism**: Tick saliva triggers IgE sensitization; subsequent red meat consumption triggers allergic reaction  
**Symptoms**: Delayed anaphylaxis (3–6 hours after eating mammalian meat/products)  
**Estimated US cases**: 450,000+ sensitized (CDC 2023 estimate)  
**Reportable**: Not currently notifiable; CDC tracking since 2021

## Tick Life Cycle and Seasonality

Most medically important ticks (e.g., *Ixodes* spp.) have a 2-year, 3-host life cycle:

```
Year 1:
  Spring/Summer  → Eggs hatch → Larvae feed on small mammals (esp. mice)
  Late Summer    → Larvae molt to nymphs → overwinter

Year 2:
  Spring/Summer  → Nymphs feed (PEAK TRANSMISSION RISK — tiny, hard to detect)
  Fall           → Nymphs molt to adults
  Fall–Winter    → Adults feed on deer; females lay eggs
```

**Nymph stage carries the highest public health risk** — they are poppy-seed sized and often feed undetected.

### Seasonal Risk Calendar (Northeast/Upper Midwest)

| Month | Stage | Activity Level | Disease Risk |
|-------|-------|---------------|-------------|
| Jan–Feb | Adult | Low (cold dormancy) | Moderate if above 35°F |
| Mar–Apr | Adult | Rising | RMSF, Lyme |
| May | Nymph emerging | **High** | **Lyme, Anaplasmosis, Babesiosis** |
| Jun–Jul | Nymph peak | **Peak** | **All Ixodes diseases** |
| Aug | Nymph declining | Moderate | Lyme, CTF |
| Sep | Adult emerging | Rising | Lyme |
| Oct–Nov | Adult peak | **High** | Lyme, RMSF |
| Dec | Adult | Moderate | Lyme (active above freezing) |

### Seasonal Risk Calendar (Rocky Mountain / Colorado)

| Month | Species Active | Diseases |
|-------|---------------|---------|
| Mar–May | *D. andersoni*, *D. variabilis* | RMSF, CTF, Tularemia |
| May–Jul | *D. andersoni* peak nymphs | **CTF peak**, RMSF |
| Jun–Aug | *I. pacificus* nymphs (western slope) | Lyme, Anaplasmosis |
| Jul–Sep | *A. americanum* (eastern plains) | Ehrlichiosis, Tularemia |
| Sep–Nov | Adult *Dermacentor* | RMSF, CTF |

## Environmental and Climatic Drivers

### Temperature
- *Ixodes scapularis* active above **4°C (39°F)**; desiccation risk above 40°C
- Warmer winters reduce off-host mortality → expanding range northward
- Growing degree days (GDD) used to model phenological timing

### Humidity and Vapor Pressure Deficit
- Ticks are highly susceptible to desiccation; off-host survival requires >80% relative humidity
- Woodland edge habitats provide microclimatic buffering
- Drought conditions reduce tick survival in exposed areas

### Habitat
- Lyme risk correlates strongly with **woodland edges**, leaf litter depth, and deer density
- *D. andersoni* prefers **mountain meadows and rocky outcroppings** (4,000–10,000 ft)
- Invasive plant species (Japanese barberry, multiflora rose) create favorable tick microhabitat

### Climate Change Projections
- *I. scapularis* range projected to expand into Canada and the Upper Midwest
- Earlier spring tick emergence (phenological advance of 2–4 weeks by 2050 under RCP 8.5)
- Extended active season increases cumulative exposure risk

## Surveillance and Monitoring Methods

### Passive Surveillance
| Method | Description | Strengths | Limitations |
|--------|-------------|-----------|-------------|
| **Case reporting** | Physician-reported notifiable diseases to state health departments → CDC ArboNET/NNDSS | Gold standard; clinical confirmation | Lag time weeks–months; under-reporting |
| **Tick submission programs** | Public submits ticks for identification/pathogen testing (e.g., TickReport, CDC TickNET) | Real-time geographic data | Biased toward known bite sites |
| **Veterinary surveillance** | Canine seroprevalence for Lyme/Ehrlichia/Anaplasma (Companion Animal Parasite Council) | High spatial resolution, real-time | Proxy measure; vaccination confounds |

### Active Field Surveillance
| Method | Description | Frequency |
|--------|-------------|-----------|
| **Drag sampling** (flannel flag dragged over vegetation) | Counts host-seeking ticks per area; identifies species and life stage | Monthly during season |
| **CO2-baited traps** | Carbon dioxide attracts questing ticks; used for *Amblyomma* surveillance | Weekly |
| **Small mammal trapping** | Collects larvae/nymphs on reservoir hosts; estimates enzootic transmission intensity | Seasonal |
| **Deer tick burdens** (from harvested deer) | Counts adult ticks on hunter-killed deer at check stations | Annual (hunting season) |

### Sentinel Animal Surveillance
- **White-tailed deer serology**: Seroprevalence for tick-borne pathogens correlates with local transmission intensity
- **Canine Lyme serology** (C6 ELISA from veterinary clinics): Available via CAPC (companion animal parasite council) at county level
- **Migratory bird tick carriage**: Passerine birds carry larval/nymphal *Ixodes* ticks long distances; surveyed at bird banding stations

### Digital and Remote Sensing Signals
| Signal | Source | Lag | Notes |
|--------|--------|-----|-------|
| Google Trends: "tick bite", "bull's eye rash", "Lyme disease" | Google Trends API | Real-time | Correlates with seasonal case incidence |
| iNaturalist *Ixodes*, *Dermacentor* observations | iNaturalist API | Real-time | Citizen science; rapid spatial expansion detection |
| TickEncounter (URI) reports | tickencounter.org | Real-time | Tick species ID reports with geolocation |
| NDVI (vegetation index) | MODIS/Landsat/Sentinel-2 | 16-day | Habitat suitability proxy |
| Land surface temperature | MODIS MOD11 | Daily | Tick activity threshold model input |
| Deer density maps | USDA Wildlife Services, state game agencies | Annual | Reservoir/host density |
| Vapor pressure deficit | NOAA PRISM, ERA5 | Daily | Off-host tick survival model |

## Early Warning Indicators

The following signals, when elevated, suggest increased tick-borne disease risk in the coming 2–6 weeks:

| Signal | Threshold | Lead Time | Disease |
|--------|-----------|-----------|---------|
| Growing degree days exceed 280 (base 0°C) | Seasonal threshold | 2–3 weeks | *Ixodes* nymph emergence |
| NDVI > 0.4 in woodland edge zones | Habitat suitability | Seasonal | General tick habitat |
| iNaturalist *Ixodes* observations surge | >50% above baseline | 1–2 weeks | Lyme, Anaplasmosis |
| Google Trends "tick bite" spike | 2x baseline | 0–1 weeks | General exposure events |
| Canine Lyme seroprevalence >5% in county | Enzootic threshold | Lagged | Human Lyme risk |
| Consecutive days >4°C after winter | Spring phenology trigger | 2–4 weeks | *Dermacentor* activity |
| Deer harvest tick burden >10 ticks/deer | High host density | Annual | Following-year risk |

## Risk Mapping Approaches

### Habitat Suitability Modelling
- **MaxEnt** or **BRT (Boosted Regression Trees)**: Species distribution modelling for tick range
- **Inputs**: NDVI, land cover class, temperature, humidity, deer density, elevation
- **Output**: Probability surface of *Ixodes* or *Dermacentor* presence

### Enzootic Risk Index
Combines:
1. Tick abundance (drag-sampling density)
2. Tick infection prevalence (pathogen testing)
3. Host density (deer, mice)
4. Human exposure index (recreational land use, population)

$$\text{ERI} = \text{Tick density} \times \text{Infection rate} \times \text{Human exposure}$$

### Climate Envelope Projections
- Project future tick range under CMIP6 SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5 scenarios
- Identify counties at risk of *I. scapularis* establishment within 10/20/30 years

## Other Trackable Rodent- and Wildlife-Associated Diseases

The same environmental and digital surveillance methods used for tick-borne diseases apply to a broader set of zoonotic diseases driven by **rodent population dynamics, habitat change, and climate cycles**. These diseases share key monitoring signals — rodent abundance, precipitation anomalies, vegetation greenness, and citizen science observations — with the AEDES framework.

### Hantavirus Pulmonary Syndrome (HPS)

**Agent**: Sin Nombre virus (SNV); also Bayou, Black Creek Canal, and other hantaviruses  
**Reservoir**: Deer mouse (*Peromyscus maniculatus*) — primary; also cotton rat, rice rat, white-footed mouse  
**Transmission**: Inhalation of aerosolized rodent urine, droppings, or saliva; NOT person-to-person  
**Range**: Western and central US; Colorado is historically one of the highest-incidence states (Four Corners region)  
**Incubation**: 1–8 weeks  
**Symptoms**: Prodrome of fever, myalgia, fatigue (days 1–5); rapid progression to respiratory failure (days 5–10)  
**Case fatality**: **35–40%** — one of the highest CFRs of any US-endemic disease  
**Annual US cases**: ~20–40 reported; about 850 total cases since 1993  
**Reportable**: Nationally notifiable  
**Seasonal pattern**: Spring–summer peak, following winter rodent population boom  

**Why it's trackable with AEDES methods:**
- Deer mouse populations surge after above-average precipitation years (El Niño/La Niña cycles), particularly following wet winters that increase vegetation and food supply
- NDVI (vegetation greenness) is a strong proxy for rodent habitat quality — high NDVI in spring predicts elevated rodent density 6–8 weeks later
- iNaturalist *Peromyscus* observations can signal unusual population activity
- Google Trends ("hantavirus", "deer mouse symptoms") correlate with case clusters
- PRISM precipitation anomalies 6–12 months prior are the best-validated early warning signal

**Key data sources**:

| Source | URL | Notes |
|--------|-----|-------|
| CDC Hantavirus case data | wonder.cdc.gov | Annual counts by state/county |
| NOAA PRISM precipitation | prism.oregonstate.edu | 6-month precipitation anomaly |
| MODIS NDVI | lpdaac.usgs.gov | Vegetation greenness (rodent habitat) |
| iNaturalist *Peromyscus* | api.inaturalist.org | Deer mouse observations |
| USDA NWRC rodent data | aphis.usda.gov | Rodent population index |

### Plague

**Agent**: *Yersinia pestis* (gram-negative bacterium)  
**Vector**: Fleas (primarily *Oropsylla montana* on prairie dogs; *Xenopsylla cheopis* on rats)  
**Reservoirs**: Prairie dogs, ground squirrels, chipmunks, wood rats  
**Transmission**: Flea bite; direct contact with infected animal; rarely respiratory (pneumonic plague)  
**Range**: Western US; Colorado, New Mexico, Arizona, California — **Colorado averages 1–4 human cases/year**  
**Incubation**: 2–8 days (bubonic); 1–4 days (pneumonic)  
**Symptoms**: Sudden fever, swollen lymph nodes (buboes); pneumonic form is rapidly fatal without treatment  
**Case fatality**: 10–15% with antibiotics; nearly 100% untreated pneumonic plague  
**Annual US cases**: ~7 (range 1–17)  
**Reportable**: Nationally notifiable; potential bioterrorism agent (Category A)  
**Seasonality**: June–September in Colorado; follows flea and prairie dog activity  

**Why it's trackable with AEDES methods:**
- Prairie dog colony die-offs (visible as vegetation change in satellite imagery and reported via citizen science) are the clearest early warning signal — mass die-offs precede human cases by weeks
- NDVI drop in prairie dog town footprints detectable via Landsat/Sentinel-2 time series
- iNaturalist and eBird: sudden absence of burrowing owls (which nest in prairie dog towns) signals colony die-off
- Colorado Parks & Wildlife reports prairie dog die-offs through a public notification system
- Flea index (fleas per host animal) from small mammal trapping predicts spillover risk

**Key data sources**:

| Source | URL | Notes |
|--------|-----|-------|
| CDC plague surveillance | cdc.gov/plague | Annual case reports |
| CDPHE plague reports | cdphe.colorado.gov | Colorado-specific, near real-time |
| Colorado Parks & Wildlife | cpw.state.co.us | Prairie dog die-off notifications |
| iNaturalist prairie dogs | api.inaturalist.org | Colony presence/absence |
| Landsat/Sentinel-2 | earthexplorer.usgs.gov | Vegetation die-off detection |

### Leptospirosis

**Agent**: *Leptospira interrogans* and other pathogenic serovars (spirochete)  
**Reservoirs**: Rats, cattle, dogs, wildlife (raccoons, skunks, opossums)  
**Transmission**: Contact with water or soil contaminated with infected animal urine; through mucous membranes or skin abrasions  
**Range**: Worldwide; US cases mostly in Hawaii, Puerto Rico, and post-flooding events nationwide  
**Incubation**: 2–30 days  
**Symptoms**: Biphasic illness — initial flu-like phase, then Weil's disease (jaundice, renal failure, hemorrhage)  
**Case fatality**: <5% mild disease; up to 40% Weil's disease  
**Annual US cases**: ~100–150 (significant under-reporting)  
**Reportable**: Nationally notifiable in some states; not uniform  
**Outbreak triggers**: Flooding events, agricultural work, water recreation after heavy rain  

**Why it's trackable with AEDES methods:**
- Flood extent mapping (Sentinel-1 SAR imagery) identifies high-risk exposure areas within days of flooding events
- Precipitation anomalies and flood forecasts (NOAA NWS) provide advance warning
- NDWI (Normalized Difference Water Index) from satellite detects standing water accumulation
- Social media and Google Trends ("leptospirosis", "rat urine water") spike after flooding events

### Rabies (Wildlife)

**Agent**: Rabies lyssavirus  
**Reservoirs**: Bats (all US states), raccoons (eastern US), skunks (central US), foxes (Alaska, SW US), coyotes (historically Texas)  
**Transmission**: Bite or scratch from infected animal; rarely mucous membrane exposure to saliva  
**Range**: Nationwide; different reservoir species dominate by region  
**Incubation**: 1 week–1 year (median ~2 months); depends on bite location and viral load  
**Case fatality**: Virtually 100% once symptomatic  
**Annual US human cases**: 1–3 (down from hundreds pre-vaccination era); bat exposure is now the primary source  
**Animal cases**: ~5,000 positive animals/year  
**Reportable**: Nationally notifiable  

**Why it's trackable with AEDES methods:**
- Bat population and roosting site data from eBird, iNaturalist, and Bat Conservation International
- Raccoon rabies epizootic front (northeastern US) moves predictably and can be tracked by case reports
- Google Trends ("bat bite", "animal bite rabies") correlate with seasonal bat activity and exposure events
- Wildlife agency reports of rabid animals (available via USDA APHIS) provide geographic risk mapping

### Summary: Applicability of AEDES Methods to These Diseases

| Disease | Key Driver | Best Remote Sensing Signal | Best Digital Signal | Lead Time |
|---------|-----------|--------------------------|---------------------|-----------|
| **Hantavirus** | Rodent abundance (precipitation-driven) | NDVI anomaly (6–8 week lag) | Google Trends, PRISM precip anomaly | 6–12 weeks |
| **Plague** | Prairie dog die-offs, flea index | Landsat NDVI drop in prairie dog towns | iNaturalist colony observations, CPW reports | 2–4 weeks |
| **Leptospirosis** | Flooding, rodent density | Sentinel-1 SAR flood extent, NDWI | NOAA flood forecasts, Google Trends | 0–1 weeks (reactive) |
| **Rabies** | Wildlife reservoir activity | Bat roost site maps, wildlife density | eBird bat reports, Google Trends | Seasonal |

All of these could be added to the AEDES framework as additional disease contexts alongside tick- and mosquito-borne diseases, using the same data pipeline architecture.
