# Mosquito-Borne Disease Surveillance

Mosquitoes are the world's deadliest animals, responsible for more than 700,000 human deaths annually through disease transmission. In the United States, mosquito-borne diseases affect tens of thousands of people each year, with West Nile Virus being the leading cause of domestically-acquired arboviral neurological disease since 1999. Climate change is expanding the geographic range of key vector species.

---

## Mosquito Species and Their Diseases

### *Aedes aegypti* — Yellow Fever Mosquito
**Range**: Tropical and subtropical; US presence in FL, TX, AZ, CA, HI; northward expansion ongoing  
**Active season**: Year-round in tropics; May–Nov in southernmost US  
**Behavior**: Day-biting; container breeder (small artificial water); highly anthropophilic  
**Diseases transmitted**:
- Dengue fever (DENV 1–4)
- Zika virus
- Chikungunya virus
- Yellow fever virus
- Mayaro virus (emerging)

### *Aedes albopictus* — Asian Tiger Mosquito
**Range**: Much of eastern US, Pacific Coast; expanding north into New England and Great Lakes  
**Active season**: May–Oct in temperate zones; year-round in south  
**Behavior**: Day-biting; container breeder; less anthropophilic than *Ae. aegypti*; overwinters as desiccation-resistant eggs  
**Diseases transmitted**:
- Dengue fever
- Chikungunya virus
- Zika virus (less efficient vector than *Ae. aegypti*)
- Eastern Equine Encephalitis (secondary vector)
- La Crosse encephalitis (secondary vector)

### *Culex pipiens* — Northern House Mosquito
**Range**: Northern and Central US  
**Active season**: Jun–Sep  
**Behavior**: Night-biting; prefers dirty, organically-enriched water (storm drains, catch basins); ornithophilic (bird-biting) with bridge feeding to humans  
**Diseases transmitted**:
- West Nile Virus (primary urban vector)
- St. Louis Encephalitis virus
- Western Equine Encephalitis virus

### *Culex quinquefasciatus* — Southern House Mosquito
**Range**: Southern and coastal US  
**Active season**: Year-round in south; Apr–Oct in transition zones  
**Behavior**: Night-biting; similar breeding to *Cx. pipiens*; ornithophilic  
**Diseases transmitted**:
- West Nile Virus (primary southern urban vector)
- St. Louis Encephalitis virus
- Lymphatic filariasis (*Wuchereria bancrofti*) — rare in US

### *Culex tarsalis* — Western Encephalitis Mosquito
**Range**: Western US, particularly irrigated agricultural areas; endemic to Colorado  
**Active season**: May–Oct  
**Behavior**: Night-biting; breeds in irrigation ditches, rice paddies, shallow freshwater; ornithophilic → mammal bridge vector  
**Diseases transmitted**:
- **West Nile Virus** (primary rural/agricultural vector in western US)
- Western Equine Encephalitis (WEE) virus
- St. Louis Encephalitis (SLE) virus

### *Culiseta melanura* — Black-Tailed Mosquito
**Range**: Eastern US; swamp habitats  
**Active season**: May–Sep  
**Behavior**: Feeds almost exclusively on birds; maintains enzootic EEE cycle  
**Diseases transmitted**:
- Eastern Equine Encephalitis (primary enzootic amplification vector)

### *Aedes triseriatus* — Eastern Tree Hole Mosquito
**Range**: Eastern and central US  
**Active season**: Jun–Sep  
**Behavior**: Tree hole and tire breeder; day-biting  
**Diseases transmitted**:
- La Crosse encephalitis (primary vector)

### *Aedes vexans* — Inland Floodwater Mosquito
**Range**: Nationwide; common after flooding events  
**Active season**: May–Oct  
**Behavior**: Nuisance biter; breeds in floodwaters; potential bridge vector  
**Diseases transmitted**:
- West Nile Virus (opportunistic secondary vector during high-density periods)

### *Anopheles* spp. — Malaria Mosquitoes
**Range**: *An. quadrimaculatus* (eastern US), *An. freeborni* (western US)  
**Diseases transmitted**:
- Malaria (*Plasmodium* spp.) — transmission risk re-emerging in US (FL, TX, MD, AR outbreaks 2023)
- Dog heartworm (not pathogenic to humans)

## Disease Reference

### West Nile Virus (WNV)
**Agent**: West Nile virus (Flavivirus)  
**Primary vectors**: *Cx. tarsalis* (west), *Cx. pipiens/quinquefasciatus* (east/south)  
**Amplifying hosts**: Corvids (crows, ravens), house finches, house sparrows  
**Transmission cycle**: Bird → mosquito → bird (enzootic); mosquito → human (dead-end host)  
**Incubation**: 2–14 days  
**Symptoms**: 80% asymptomatic; ~20% West Nile fever; <1% neuroinvasive (encephalitis, meningitis, acute flaccid paralysis)  
**Case fatality**: ~10% of neuroinvasive cases; ~1,800 deaths since 1999  
**Annual US cases**: 500–7,500 neuroinvasive reported; estimated 2–3 million total infections/year  
**Reportable**: Nationally notifiable (ArboNET)  
**Blood supply risk**: Can be transfusion-transmitted; blood donations screened by PCR  
**Monitoring signals**: Dead crow/corvid reports, mosquito pool positivity rates, equine cases (sentinel), weather (heat + drought concentrate birds/mosquitoes at shared water sources)

### Dengue Fever
**Agent**: Dengue virus (DENV 1–4, Flavivirus)  
**Primary vectors**: *Ae. aegypti*, *Ae. albopictus*  
**Transmission**: Human-amplified; no reservoir in US wildlife  
**Incubation**: 4–10 days  
**Symptoms**: High fever, severe headache ("breakbone fever"), retro-orbital pain, myalgia, rash  
**Severe dengue**: Plasma leakage, hemorrhage, shock — CFR <1% with treatment, up to 20% without  
**US cases**: ~5,000–8,000 travel-related/year; local transmission in FL, TX, HI, PR  
**Reportable**: Nationally notifiable  
**Serotype risk**: Prior infection with one serotype increases severe disease risk upon second infection (antibody-dependent enhancement)  
**Monitoring signals**: Google Trends (travel-associated), airport passenger flows, *Ae. aegypti* container index (# positive containers / # inspected × 100), climate (temperature, precipitation)

### Eastern Equine Encephalitis (EEE)
**Agent**: Eastern equine encephalitis virus (Alphavirus)  
**Enzootic vector**: *Cs. melanura* (bird-to-bird)  
**Bridge vectors**: *Ae. sollicitans*, *Ae. vexans*, *Ae. albopictus*  
**Amplifying hosts**: Wild birds (passerines, wading birds)  
**Incubation**: 4–10 days  
**Symptoms**: Fever progressing to encephalitis  
**Case fatality**: **30–40%** (highest CFR of any mosquito-borne encephalitis in US)  
**Annual US cases**: 5–10 (rare; sporadic outbreaks; 38 cases in 2019 outbreak)  
**Long-term effects**: >50% of survivors have significant neurological sequelae  
**Reportable**: Nationally notifiable  
**Sentinel surveillance**: Horse cases precede human cases by 1–2 weeks

### Western Equine Encephalitis (WEE)
**Agent**: Western equine encephalitis virus (Alphavirus)  
**Vectors**: *Cx. tarsalis*  
**Range**: Western US, especially irrigated valleys; no confirmed US cases since 1994  
**Status**: Possibly locally extinct in US; still monitored  
**Monitoring signals**: Sentinel chicken seroconversion, *Cx. tarsalis* pool testing

### St. Louis Encephalitis (SLE)
**Agent**: St. Louis encephalitis virus (Flavivirus)  
**Vectors**: *Cx. pipiens*, *Cx. quinquefasciatus*, *Cx. tarsalis*  
**Range**: Nationwide; sporadic epidemics  
**Symptoms**: Mostly subclinical; neurological disease in elderly  
**Case fatality**: 5–20% of neuroinvasive cases  
**Annual US cases**: <10 most years; periodic outbreaks  
**Reportable**: Nationally notifiable

### La Crosse Encephalitis (LACV)
**Agent**: La Crosse virus (Orthobunyavirus)  
**Primary vector**: *Ae. triseriatus* (tree hole mosquito)  
**Amplifying hosts**: Chipmunks, squirrels  
**Range**: Eastern and central US; Appalachian region  
**Incubation**: 5–15 days  
**Symptoms**: Pediatric encephalitis (primarily children <16 years)  
**Case fatality**: <1%; seizures and neurological sequelae in ~25% of encephalitis cases  
**Annual US cases**: 50–150 reported  
**Reportable**: Nationally notifiable

### Zika Virus
**Agent**: Zika virus (Flavivirus)  
**Primary vectors**: *Ae. aegypti*, *Ae. albopictus*  
**Transmission routes**: Mosquito bite, sexual transmission, maternal-fetal  
**Incubation**: 3–14 days  
**Symptoms**: 80% asymptomatic; mild fever, rash, conjunctivitis, arthralgia  
**Severe outcomes**: Congenital Zika syndrome (microcephaly, brain malformations), Guillain-Barré syndrome  
**US status**: Travel-associated cases; local transmission FL and TX during 2016 outbreak  
**Reportable**: Nationally notifiable  
**Monitoring signals**: Travel alerts, *Ae. aegypti* distribution, Google Trends, airport passenger flows from endemic regions

### Chikungunya Virus
**Agent**: Chikungunya virus (Alphavirus)  
**Primary vectors**: *Ae. aegypti*, *Ae. albopictus*  
**Incubation**: 2–12 days  
**Symptoms**: Sudden fever, severe joint pain (can persist months–years), rash  
**Case fatality**: <1%  
**US status**: Travel-associated; limited local transmission in FL (2014, 2023)  
**Reportable**: Nationally notifiable  
**Monitoring signals**: Similar to dengue; *Ae. albopictus* distribution key for temperate spread risk

### Malaria
**Agent**: *Plasmodium falciparum*, *P. vivax*, *P. malariae*, *P. ovale*, *P. knowlesi*  
**Primary US vectors**: *An. quadrimaculatus*, *An. freeborni*  
**US status**: Re-emerging; 2023 outbreaks in FL, TX, MD, AR (first locally-acquired cases in 20 years)  
**Incubation**: 7–30 days (P. vivax can relapse months later from liver hypnozoites)  
**Symptoms**: Cyclical fever, chills, headache, nausea; severe: cerebral malaria, organ failure  
**Case fatality**: <0.1% with treatment; up to 20% untreated severe malaria  
**Annual US cases**: ~2,000 (mostly travel-related)  
**Reportable**: Nationally notifiable

## Mosquito Life Cycle and Seasonality

Mosquitoes have a complete metamorphosis (holometabolous) life cycle:

```
Egg → Larva (4 instars, aquatic) → Pupa (aquatic) → Adult (terrestrial/aerial)

Duration at 25°C: ~10–14 days (egg to adult)
Duration at 15°C: ~25–30 days (slower development)
Below 10°C: Development halts; adults may overwinter as mated females
```

**Temperature determines everything** — development rate, biting frequency, pathogen extrinsic incubation period (EIP), and adult survival.

### Extrinsic Incubation Period (EIP)

The time for pathogen to replicate within the mosquito to infectious levels:

| Pathogen | EIP at 25°C | EIP at 30°C | Minimum Temp |
|----------|------------|------------|-------------|
| West Nile Virus | 14 days | 7 days | ~14°C |
| Dengue (DENV-2) | 12 days | 7 days | ~16°C |
| Zika | 15 days | 8 days | ~18°C |
| Chikungunya | 10 days | 5 days | ~16°C |
| *Plasmodium vivax* | 16 days | 9 days | ~16°C |

### Seasonal Risk Calendar (Continental US)

| Month | Region | Primary Vector Active | Primary Risks |
|-------|--------|-----------------------|---------------|
| Jan–Mar | South (FL, TX, LA) | *Ae. aegypti*, *Cx. quinquefasciatus* | Dengue (travel), WNV sporadic |
| Apr–May | SE US, expanding north | *Aedes* spp., *Culex* spp. emerging | Dengue risk rising |
| Jun–Jul | Nationwide | Peak *Culex*; *Aedes* active | **WNV, La Crosse, EEE** |
| Aug–Sep | Nationwide | **All vectors at peak** | **WNV peak, EEE outbreak risk** |
| Oct | South; declining elsewhere | *Culex* declining | Late WNV cases |
| Nov–Dec | South only | *Ae. aegypti* persisting | Travel-associated diseases |

### Colorado-Specific Seasonality

| Month | Vector Status | Primary Risk |
|-------|--------------|-------------|

| Jun | *Cx. tarsalis* + *Ae. vexans* active | **WNV surveillance begins** |

## Environmental and Climatic Drivers

### Temperature
- **Optimal development**: 25–30°C for most *Culex* and *Aedes*
- **Biting rate**: Increases exponentially with temperature; halves for every 10°C drop
- **Key threshold**: Above 14°C for WNV transmission competence; above 16°C for dengue
- **Heat waves**: Force birds and mosquitoes to share limited water sources → amplified WNV transmission

### Precipitation and Standing Water
- Container-breeding species (*Ae. aegypti*, *Ae. albopictus*): Heavy rain creates breeding opportunities
- Floodwater breeders (*Ae. vexans*): Population explosions following flooding
- Drought effect on WNV: Concentrates birds and *Cx. tarsalis* at remaining water bodies → amplification
- "Drought followed by rain" pattern linked to WNV outbreaks in Colorado and Great Plains

### Urbanization
- Urban heat islands extend mosquito season and expand range
- Storm drain infrastructure provides year-round *Culex* breeding
- Impervious surfaces alter hydrology, creating pooling

### Land Use and Water Infrastructure
- Agricultural irrigation canals (Colorado): Primary *Cx. tarsalis* larval habitat
- Urban tree canopy: Shading reduces larval development temperatures
- Wetland presence/absence: Affects *Cs. melanura* (EEE), *An. quadrimaculatus* (malaria)

## Surveillance and Monitoring Methods

### Passive Surveillance
| Method | Description | Strengths | Limitations |
|--------|-------------|-----------|-------------|
| **Human case reporting** | Physician-reported to state health dept → ArboNET | Confirmed human impact | 2–6 week lag; under-reported |
| **Dead bird reporting** (WNV) | Public reports dead corvids; tested for WNV | Early warning 1–2 weeks before human cases | Reporting fatigue; crow population decline |
| **Equine case reporting** | Horse WNV/EEE/WEE cases reported by vets | Sentinels precede human cases by 1–2 weeks | Vaccination confounds signal |

### Active Entomological Surveillance
| Method | Target | Description |
|--------|--------|-------------|
| **CO2-baited light traps** (CDC trap) | Host-seeking adult females | Standard for WNV/encephalitis surveillance; counts by species |
| **BG-Sentinel traps** | *Aedes* species | Lures *Ae. aegypti*, *Ae. albopictus* with heat + BG-Lure |
| **Gravid traps** (Reiter trap) | Egg-laying *Culex* females | Highest WNV infection rates (engorged, seeking oviposition) |
| **Ovitrap/oviposition traps** | *Aedes* eggs | Paddle collects eggs for counting and ID; cheap, deployable at scale |
| **Dipping** (larval sampling) | Larvae in water | Dipper counts instars; calculates larval indices |
| **Container index surveys** | *Ae. aegypti*, *Ae. albopictus* | Stegomyia indices (House Index, Container Index, Breteau Index) |

### Sentinel Surveillance
| Sentinel | Method | Lead Time vs. Human Cases |
|---------|--------|--------------------------|
| **Sentinel chicken flocks** | Weekly blood draws; ELISA for WNV/SLE/WEE/EEE IgM | 2–3 weeks |
| **Wild bird WNV mortality** | Dead crow counts per surveillance area | 1–2 weeks |
| **Mosquito pool testing** | RT-PCR on pooled female mosquitoes; calculate MIR | 1–2 weeks |

**Minimum Infection Rate (MIR)**:
$$\text{MIR} = \frac{\text{Positive pools}}{\text{Total mosquitoes tested}} \times 1000$$

Action thresholds vary by jurisdiction; many programs alert at MIR ≥ 1.0.

| Signal | Source | Update Frequency | Utility |
|--------|--------|-----------------|---------|
| Google Trends: "mosquito bite", "West Nile", "dengue" | Google Trends API | Real-time | Correlates with case incidence; 1–2 week lead |
| iNaturalist *Aedes*, *Culex* observations | iNaturalist API | Real-time | Range expansion detection |
| Mosquito Alert (citizen science) | mosquitoalert.com/en/api/ | Real-time | *Ae. albopictus/aegypti* presence reports with photos |

## Early Warning Indicators

| Signal | Threshold | Lead Time | Disease |
|--------|-----------|-----------|---------|
| *Cx. tarsalis* trap counts >50/trap/night | Elevated activity | 2–3 weeks | WNV |
| MIR (pool infection rate) ≥ 1.0 | Transmission occurring | 1–2 weeks | WNV, SLE |
| Sentinel chicken seroconversion ≥ 1 per site | Local transmission | 2–3 weeks | WNV, EEE |
| Dead crow density >1 per km² | Epizootic in birds | 1–2 weeks | WNV |
| Mean daily temperature >18°C for 14+ days | EIP shortens | Seasonal | All flaviviruses |
| Accumulated degree days (base 14°C) > 200 | *Cx. tarsalis* peak | 2 weeks | WNV |
| Google Trends "West Nile" 2x baseline | Human awareness/cases | 0–1 weeks | WNV |
| Drought index (PDSI) < -2 + heat wave | Bird/mosquito concentration | Immediate | WNV (western US) |
| *Ae. aegypti* Breteau Index > 5 | Dengue outbreak threshold (WHO) | 2–4 weeks | Dengue, Zika, Chikungunya |
| Horse cases reported | Equine sentinels | 1–2 weeks | WNV, EEE, WEE |

## Risk Scoring Framework

### West Nile Virus Risk Score
A composite weekly index combining entomological, environmental, and epidemiological signals:

$$R_{WNV} = w_1 \cdot \text{MIR} + w_2 \cdot \text{TrapCount} + w_3 \cdot T_{mean} + w_4 \cdot \text{BirdDeaths} + w_5 \cdot \text{ChickenConv}$$

Where weights $w_1$–$w_5$ are calibrated on historical outbreak data.

**Tier thresholds** (example — calibrate per jurisdiction):
- **Green** (Baseline): R < 2.0
- **Yellow** (Elevated): 2.0 ≤ R < 5.0
- **Orange** (High): 5.0 ≤ R < 8.0
- **Red** (Outbreak risk): R ≥ 8.0

### Dengue Epidemic Potential Index (EPI)
Uses temperature-dependent transmission probability:

$$EPI = \frac{a^2 \cdot b \cdot c \cdot e^{-\mu/n}}{\mu \cdot r}$$

Where:
- $a$ = biting rate (temperature-dependent)
- $b$ = mosquito-to-human transmission probability
- $c$ = human-to-mosquito transmission probability
- $\mu$ = adult mosquito mortality rate
- $n$ = extrinsic incubation period (temperature-dependent)
- $r$ = human recovery rate

## Container Index Monitoring (*Aedes* spp.)

For *Ae. aegypti* and *Ae. albopictus* surveillance, the WHO Stegomyia indices quantify larval infestation:

| Index | Formula | Action Threshold |
|-------|---------|-----------------|
| **House Index (HI)** | (Houses with Aedes / Houses inspected) × 100 | HI > 1 = dengue outbreak risk |
| **Container Index (CI)** | (Positive containers / Containers inspected) × 100 | CI > 3 = elevated risk |
| **Breteau Index (BI)** | (Positive containers / Houses inspected) × 100 | BI > 5 = high risk (WHO) |

## Comparison: Tick vs. Mosquito Surveillance

| Factor | Tick Surveillance | Mosquito Surveillance |
|--------|------------------|----------------------|
| **Detection method** | Drag sampling, tick submissions | Trap counts, pool testing |
| **Sentinel animals** | Dogs (serology), deer (tick burden) | Chickens (seroconversion), horses (clinical) |
| **Key environmental drivers** | Temperature, humidity, NDVI, deer density | Temperature, precipitation, standing water, drought |
| **Early warning lead time** | 2–4 weeks (habitat/phenology) | 1–2 weeks (MIR, sentinel conversion) |
| **Key digital signal** | iNaturalist tick observations | Dead bird reports, trap data |
| **Season** | Spring–Fall (some year-round) | Summer–Fall (Jun–Oct in CO) |
| **Google Trends signal** | "tick bite", "Lyme disease", "bull's eye rash" | "mosquito", "West Nile", "dengue" |
